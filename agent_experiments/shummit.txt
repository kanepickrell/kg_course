Shummit Proposal: Semantic AI Platform 

Problem Statement 

Many organizations report that a large share of their AI development effort is consumed by 
data preparation, integration, and aligning data across teams—not by developing models 
or intelligence. According to BCG research, organizations following the “10-20-70” 
principle spend about 10% of their effort on algorithms, 20% on technology & data, and 
70% on people, process, and cultural transformation. 

Teams across departments often build AI applications in isolation; they each solve 
recurring data connectivity, metadata, schema mismatch, or discovery challenges, and 
insights tend to stay within domain silos. The issue is not always lack of data, but lack of 
discoverability, shared vocabulary, or cross-team connection of data and semantic 
definitions. 

Proposed Solution 

We propose to build a Semantic AI Infrastructure Platform which enables teams to: 

•  Define shared business concepts once (e.g., Person, Project, System, Event) using 

web/semantic standards (e.g. JSON-LD contexts, RDF, etc.). 

•  Map their existing data to those shared concept definitions while keeping existing 

systems and workflows. 

•  Publish metadata/data mappings so that AI applications or analytic pipelines can 
discover and integrate relevant data across teams automatically without ad-hoc 
custom integration. 

The idea is to shift some integration work outward: data-producing/publishing teams take 
responsibility for mapping; the system provides discoverability and linking. 

Evidence & Academic Merit 

Below are studies and observations that support technical feasibility, benefits, and known 
challenges; where precise metrics are unavailable, language has been generalized. 

•  Technical Feasibility & Case Studies 

1.  MapSDI: A Scaled-up Semantic Data Integration Framework (Jozashoori & 

Vidal, 2019) demonstrates that using mapping rules and preprocessing steps 
(projection, deduplication) guided by semantic mappings can reduce 

knowledge graph creation time by an order of magnitude vs naïve 
approaches  

2.  Knowledge Graph Induction enabling Recommending and Trend Analysis: A 
Corporate Research Community Use Case (IBM Research) – shows how 
structured + textual data from different systems can be unified under a 
knowledge graph, and supports recommendation & analytics use cases 

3.  BCG’s “Where’s the Value in AI?” (2024) survey of many companies shows 
that only ~25-30% are moving beyond proofs of concept, with many still 
struggling to scale value; leaders in AI mature in both technical capability 
and organizational change 

4.  The “10-20-70” allocation model from BCG is repeatedly cited in recent AI 

strategy/consulting work, indicating that many organizations recognize that 
the people/process dimension is often the largest and hardest to address.  

•  Benefits in Accuracy, Discovery, & Cross-Domain Use 

1.  A Benchmark to Understand the Role of Knowledge Graphs on LLM’s 

Accuracy for Question Answering on Enterprise SQL Databases (Sequeda, 
Allemang, Jacob, et al., 2023) shows that for question answering tasks over 
an enterprise SQL schema, using a knowledge-graph representation + 
ontology + mappings increases accuracy substantially vs zero-shot LLM over 
raw SQL tables (≈ 16% → ≈ 54%).  

2.  JEL: Applying End-to-End Neural Entity Linking in JPMorgan Chase shows 

practical deployment of knowledge graph / entity linking in a large enterprise 
setting, with cross-system integration and use in risk/fraud/advice systems. 

• 

Industry / Organizational Observations & Challenges 

1.  Many case studies (from e.g. Enterprise Knowledge) show organizations 

struggling with data discovery, inconsistent metadata, siloed systems, and 
decentralized content or practice, which semantic layers / knowledge graph / 
graph-based metadata systems help to address.  

2.  Industrial enterprise knowledge-graph development research (e.g. Adoption 

of knowledge-graph best development practices (Jawad et al., 
ScienceDirect, 2023)) documents that challenges include data quality, 
scalability of graph creation, governance, and tooling. 

•  Proposal Limitations 

o  There are very few publicly published large-scale ROI studies that directly 
compare, say, “semantic infrastructure + cross-team discoverability” vs 
“traditional ETL / integration approaches” in exactly similar settings. 

o  Many case studies are qualitative (improved insight, reduced duplicated 
effort) rather than fully quantified (dollar savings, latency improvements 
across the board). 

o  Performance metrics (for large/enterprise datasets) are often proprietary or 

not disclosed; so setting expectations must be cautious. 

Technical Foundation 

This doesn’t have to be the way forward but platform may build on these tools and 
frameworks: 

• 

JSON-LD (contexts, types) and RDF (including named graphs or similar concepts) 
are mature standards with broad adoption. 

•  Existing architectures / frameworks (e.g. mapping-rule frameworks like MapSDI) 
show that large heterogeneous sources can be integrated through semantic 
mappings. 

•  There are enterprise tools / graph databases (Neo4j, Stardog, GraphDB, Apache 
Jena, etc.) that support RDF/triples, graph querying, reasoning, etc. Many case 
studies use such tools. 

•  Performance / scale issues are tractable but non-trivial; approaches often require 

preprocessing, deduplication, incremental updates, good indexing, and governance 
to keep things maintainable. 

Two-Week Deliverable 

Week 1: Infrastructure Foundation 

•  Schema/ontology/context registry using JSON-LD/RDF standards 

•  Data / dataset registration API so teams can expose metadata / data mappings to 

the shared schema. 

•  Graph database instance with sample dataset(s) and ability to store named graphs 

or graph fragments with connections. 

Week 2: Cross-Team AI Demonstration 

•  Simulating multiple organizational teams for a generic organization (i.e. HR, IT, 

Operations) mapping to shared concepts. 

•  Building a sample AI application or analytic dashboard that can use federated 

queries (SPARQL over multiple named graphs or datasets) to answer cross-domain 
questions that previously were difficult or impossible. 

•  Example queries: e.g., “How do system access patterns correlate with project 

outcomes across departments?”, “Are there patterns in employee performance, 
resource usage, and event logs that cross teams?” etc. 

Success Metrics & Realistic Expectations 

Technical / Performance Metrics 

•  Time to register a dataset with shared vocabulary (ideally low, but define realistic 

target depending on complexity) 

•  Discovery coverage: proportion of relevant cross-team datasets that are 

discoverable via the shared vocabulary / schema 

•  Query performance: federated or combined cross-team queries should run within 

acceptable latency (e.g. under some threshold, though in prototype settings may be 
higher) 

Organizational / Adoption Metrics 

•  Stakeholder readiness and willingness to map to shared concepts 

•  Consistency / quality of mappings / metadata (data validation) 

•  Usability of tools (how easily teams discover data, how intuitive the mapping 

schema registry etc.) 

Risk Mitigation & Organizational Readiness 

•  Known key challenges: governance, cultural resistance, lack of clarity in benefit, 
misalignment between domain teams, data quality, domain-specific terminology 
mismatches, etc. 

•  Mitigations: 

1.  Start with willing, early adopter teams rather than trying to force whole org 

adoption. 

2.  Focus on compelling use cases that deliver visible value early (e.g. 

report/dashboard, cross-team insights) rather than architectures alone. 

3.  Secure executive sponsorship / leadership endorsement, to align incentives 

and resource allocation. 

4.  Build or adopt data governance frameworks / schema‐management roles 

early (taxonomy/ontology stewards). 

Strategic Impact & Scope 

•  The prototype aims to test whether adding more teams increases the “network 

effect” of semantic infrastructure: each new team that maps its data adds value for 
others. 

•  The goal is technical proof of concept and organizational value exploration, without 

overpromising large ROI until sufficient scale and maturity. 

•  Because many of the benefits are qualitative or indirect (reduced duplication, faster 
discovery, more consistent definitions, ability to reuse data/models), expectations 
should include both qualitative feedback and early quantitative indicators (e.g. 
speed ups in time to insight, reduction in repeated integration effort).
